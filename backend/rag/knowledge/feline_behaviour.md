## Overview
section: overview

Behavioural problems are among the leading reasons cats are surrendered to shelters or euthanised. Yet the majority of feline behaviour problems are predictable, understandable, and treatable when the owner understands the cat's fundamental nature. Cats are solitary hunters that form complex social structures when forced to cohabit. They have a strong need for predictability, territory, and control of their environment. Most "problem behaviours" are normal cat behaviours expressed in contexts that are incompatible with human expectations — the goal of treatment is to modify the environment and household management, not simply to punish the cat.

## Litter Box Avoidance — Inappropriate Elimination
section: causes

The most common feline behaviour complaint. Before attributing it to a behavioural cause, always rule out medical causes first — urinary tract disease (FLUTD, UTI, cystitis), constipation, diarrhea, arthritis (making it difficult to step into the box), or cognitive dysfunction in senior cats.

**Medical causes MUST be ruled out first.** A cat that was using its litter box reliably for years and suddenly stops is more likely to have a medical problem than a behavioural one.

**Behavioural causes and solutions:**

**Litter box type and size:**
- Many cats prefer uncovered boxes — a covered box retains odour and can feel threatening
- The box should be 1.5 times the length of the cat — most commercial boxes are too small for large cats
- Self-cleaning boxes are tolerated by some cats but frightening to others

**Litter substrate preference:**
- Most cats prefer fine, unscented, soft, clumping clay litter at a depth of 3–5 cm
- Strongly scented litters, crystals, and newspaper pellets are often rejected
- If you switch litter brands, do it gradually — mix new with old over 2 weeks

**Cleanliness:**
- Scoop the litter box at least once daily — cats will avoid a dirty box. A clean nose will detect a used box after a single deposit
- Full litter change and box wash with mild soap (not bleach) weekly

**Number and placement:**
- The rule: minimum one box per cat, plus one extra (e.g., 3 cats = 4 boxes)
- Place boxes in quiet, accessible, private areas — not next to noisy appliances or in high-traffic areas
- Never place food and water next to the litter box — cats will not eliminate near their feeding area
- Provide boxes on every floor of a multi-storey home
- Senior cats with arthritis need low-entry boxes — cut an entrance in one side of a high-sided box

**Stress-related avoidance:**
- Multi-cat conflict, a new pet, home renovation, or a new baby can cause sudden litter box avoidance
- One cat may guard the litter box, preventing others from using it (resource guarding) — solution: more boxes in different locations

**Re-training:**
- Confine the cat to a single room with everything it needs including the litter box
- Once using reliably, gradually expand access to the rest of the home
- Clean soiled areas thoroughly with an enzymatic cleaner (e.g., Nature's Miracle) — regular cleaners leave residual scent that attracts repeat soiling

## Urine Spraying (Marking)
section: causes

Spraying is a normal communication behaviour in cats — it is distinct from inappropriate elimination. The cat stands, backs up to a vertical surface, raises its tail (which may quiver), and deposits a small amount of urine. Both intact and neutered cats spray, though intact males do so most frequently and intensely.

**Triggers:**
- Presence of outdoor cats visible through windows or doors
- Multi-cat household conflict or resource competition
- A new pet, baby, or person in the home
- New furniture or objects (especially with foreign smells)
- Anxiety and generalised stress

**Solutions:**
- Neutering eliminates spraying in about 90% of intact male cats if done before the behaviour is established
- Feliway MultiCat diffuser — synthetic cat pheromone that reduces inter-cat tension
- Block visual access to outdoor cats (frosted window film on lower panes)
- Provide more vertical space, hiding spots, and separate resource stations (food, water, litter boxes in different areas) to reduce inter-cat competition
- Clean marked areas with enzymatic cleaner — regular cleaners do not neutralise the pheromones that attract re-marking
- Prescription medication for severe anxiety: fluoxetine (Prozac), clomipramine, or buspirone

## Inter-Cat Aggression
section: causes

Cats are not inherently social species. When forced to share a home, conflict can range from subtle (staring, blocking access to resources) to serious (outright fighting with bite wounds and abscess formation).

**Types:**
- **Status-related aggression:** One cat maintains access to resources at the expense of others through posturing or overt attack
- **Redirected aggression:** A cat sees something exciting or threatening through a window (a bird, a stray cat) and redirects its arousal onto the nearest cat or person
- **Play aggression:** Most common in single kittens or young cats with insufficient interactive play — they treat humans or other cats as prey
- **Fear-induced aggression:** A cat that feels trapped or cornered will bite and scratch

**Management:**
- Separate cats that are actively fighting — provide completely separate resource sets
- Reintroduce using a gradual scent-exchange protocol (swap bedding, feed on opposite sides of a closed door, use a baby gate with a visual barrier initially)
- Feliway MultiCat reduces inter-cat tension
- If a cat was attacked suddenly by another cat that was watching something stimulating through a window — the trigger was redirected aggression. Separate them and allow 24–48 hours of complete isolation before reintroduction
- Neuter all cats — intact males fight more intensely and cause more severe wounds

## Hiding and Sudden Change in Personality
section: causes

A cat that suddenly hides, withdraws, or becomes uncharacteristically quiet may be ill. **This is often the first sign of significant illness in cats** — pain and illness trigger a withdrawal instinct.

Always investigate the following before attributing hiding to anxiety:
- Dental pain (tooth resorption, abscess, gingivostomatitis)
- Internal pain (pancreatitis, UTI, constipation)
- Systemic illness (CKD, hyperthyroidism, anaemia)
- Cognitive dysfunction syndrome (CDS — feline dementia) in senior cats
- Post-injection site sarcoma (a form of cancer at vaccine or injection sites) — particularly if you notice a swelling at a previous injection site

If hiding is accompanied by changes in eating, drinking, weight, grooming habits, or litter box use, a thorough veterinary examination is essential before assuming the cause is purely behavioural.

**Providing for a naturally shy or anxious cat:**
- Multiple hiding spots at various heights — cardboard boxes, cat tunnels, covered beds
- Feliway Classic diffuser in the cat's main living area
- Do not force interaction — let the cat approach on its own terms
- Provide elevated perches — cats feel safer when they can observe from above
- Consistent routine — predictability reduces anxiety significantly

## Night-time Vocalisation
section: causes

Cats that suddenly become very vocal, especially at night, warrant medical investigation before a behavioural diagnosis:
- **Hyperthyroidism:** Increased vocalisation and restlessness, especially in older cats
- **Hypertension:** Can cause neurological signs including vocalisation
- **Pain:** Arthritis or internal pain worsens with inactivity at night
- **Cognitive Dysfunction Syndrome (CDS):** Feline dementia causes disorientation and vocalisation, especially when the cat wakes in darkness and doesn't know where it is
- **Deafness:** Deaf cats vocalise more loudly because they cannot modulate their own volume

**Management of CDS (senior cats):**
- Night lights to reduce disorientation
- Keep the cat's sleeping area close to the owner's room
- Prescription supplements (selegiline/Anipryl in some countries) or diet (Hill's b/d) may slow progression
- Gabapentin at night for anxious elderly cats

## Scratching Behaviour
section: prevention

Scratching is a completely normal and necessary behaviour — cats scratch to remove the outer sheaths of their claws, to stretch, and to leave visual and scent marks. Redirecting scratching is far more effective than punishment.

- Provide at least two scratching posts of different materials (sisal rope, cardboard, carpet) — one must be tall enough for the cat to stretch fully (minimum 60–70 cm)
- Place posts where the cat already wants to scratch — near furniture it has used, near sleeping areas (cats scratch when they wake)
- Never punish scratching — this increases anxiety and does not eliminate the behaviour
- Nail caps (Soft Paws) are a humane temporary solution — applied over the claw tips every 4–6 weeks
- Declawing (onychectomy) is illegal in many countries and considered inhumane by the AVMA and WSAVA — it involves amputation of the last bone of each toe, causes chronic pain, and increases biting behaviour

## When to See a Vet for Behavioural Issues
section: emergency

Seek veterinary attention promptly if:
- Any sudden change in behaviour (hiding, aggression, vocalisation) in a previously normal cat — assume medical cause until proven otherwise.
- Bite wounds from cat-cat fights — abscess develops rapidly within 2–3 days and requires veterinary drainage and antibiotics.
- Self-mutilation — a cat that is obsessively biting or chewing at its own skin or tail requires both medical and behavioural investigation.
- Aggression is escalating and people or other pets are being injured.
