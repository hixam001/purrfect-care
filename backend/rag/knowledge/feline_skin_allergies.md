# Feline Skin Conditions — Allergies, Dermatitis, and Coat Problems

## Overview
section: overview

Skin and coat problems are among the most common reasons cats visit the vet. The skin is the largest organ of the body and reflects both local disease and systemic illness. Cats present with a limited repertoire of skin responses — itching (pruritus), hair loss (alopecia), crusting (miliary dermatitis), skin thickening (lichenification), and sores or ulcers — regardless of the underlying cause. This makes diagnosis challenging and often requires a systematic approach to identify the primary trigger.

---

## Flea Allergy Dermatitis (FAD)
section: causes

The most common skin condition in cats worldwide. Even a single flea bite can trigger an intense allergic reaction in a sensitised cat — you don't need to see fleas to suspect flea allergy.

**Signs:**
- Intense scratching, particularly over the lower back, rump, and base of the tail
- Tiny red-brown crusts scattered across the back and belly (miliary dermatitis)
- Hair loss and scabbing at the lower back — often a "barbered" appearance where the fur is broken off short
- The cat may be excessively grooming, pulling out fur in clumps
- You may see flea dirt (black specks that turn red when wet on white tissue paper) in the coat

**Treatment:** Aggressive, sustained flea control for ALL pets in the household and treatment of the home environment. A single missed flea can re-trigger the allergy. Modern effective flea treatments: isoxazolines (Bravecto, NexGard Combo), selamectin (Revolution), and fluralaner. Veterinary treatment may also include short-course steroids to break the itch cycle.

## Food Allergy and Food Intolerance
section: causes

Food allergies in cats develop against the protein source in the diet — the most common triggers are chicken, beef, fish, and dairy. These are all common ingredients in standard cat food, which explains why the allergy develops over years of exposure.

**Signs (distinguishing features):**
- Non-seasonal itching — food allergy itches year-round, unlike environmental allergy which is worse in pollen season
- Itching around the head, face, and neck is particularly suggestive of food allergy
- Recurrent ear infections (yeast or bacteria) alongside skin issues
- Gastrointestinal signs in some cats: vomiting, diarrhoea, increased frequency of defecation
- No response to steroid treatment (steroids usually help environmental allergy but often not food allergy)

**Diagnosis:** An 8–12 week strict elimination diet trial using a novel protein (a protein the cat has never eaten before, e.g., rabbit, venison, kangaroo, or a hydrolysed prescription diet). The cat must eat absolutely nothing else — no treats, no flavoured medications, no table scraps.

**Treatment:** Permanent avoidance of the identified allergen. There is no other cure.

## Environmental Allergy (Atopic Dermatitis)
section: causes

Environmental atopy in cats is caused by inhaled or contact allergens — house dust mites, mould spores, grass, tree, and weed pollens. Cats rarely develop hay fever like humans; instead, they itch.

**Signs:**
- Seasonal worsening (pollen-related) or year-round (dust mite-related)
- Symmetrical hair loss from over-grooming — abdomen, inner thighs, and flanks are common sites
- Eosinophilic granuloma complex: a spectrum of skin lesions unique to cats including raised, yellowish-orange linear plaques on the thighs, "rodent ulcers" (indolent ulcers) on the upper lip, and raised pink plaques on the skin
- Recurrent ear problems

**Treatment:** Allergen identification via intradermal or serology testing, allergen-specific immunotherapy (desensitisation injections or sublingual drops), anti-itch medications (oclacitinib/Apoquel, lokivetmab/Cytopoint for cats, modified cyclosporine/Atopica).

## Ringworm (Dermatophytosis)
section: causes

Despite the name, ringworm is not a worm — it is a fungal infection (Microsporum canis or Trichophyton species). It is very common in kittens and cats from shelters and highly contagious to other cats, dogs, and people.

**Signs in cats:**
- Circular, scaly patches of hair loss, often with broken-off hairs at the edges
- Lesions often on the head, ears, forelimbs, and paws
- Some cats are asymptomatic carriers — appear normal but spread the infection
- Mild itching in some cats; others are unbothered
- The classic "ring" appearance is more reliably seen in human infections

**Diagnosis:** Fungal culture (the gold standard), Wood's lamp (fluorescence, but not all strains fluoresce), and PCR.
**Treatment:** Antifungal shampoo and lime sulphur dips (messy but very effective), oral antifungals (itraconazole or terbinafine), and rigorous environmental decontamination (vacuuming, bleach diluted 1:10 on hard surfaces). Treatment typically lasts 6–8 weeks minimum.
**Zoonotic:** Yes — humans develop itchy, ring-shaped lesions, typically on the arms and face. Wear gloves when handling affected cats.

## Psychogenic Alopecia (Over-Grooming)
section: causes

Some cats develop a compulsive grooming habit in response to stress or anxiety, resulting in symmetrical, diffuse hair loss over the belly, inner thighs, and flanks. This is a diagnosis of exclusion — all physical causes of pruritus must first be ruled out.

**Common triggers:** A new pet, a new baby, building work, a changed routine, multi-cat household conflict.
**Signs:** Large areas of very short or absent fur; the skin underneath is usually normal (no redness, scaling); the cat is sometimes seen grooming excessively but owners often miss it.
**Treatment:** Identify and reduce the stressor; environmental enrichment; pheromone therapy (Feliway); in severe cases, anti-anxiety medication (fluoxetine, gabapentin, clomipramine).

## When to See a Vet
section: emergency

See a vet urgently if:
- The cat has open sores, weeping wounds, or deeply infected skin lesions.
- There is rapid spread of a skin condition in a multi-pet household (possible ringworm).
- The cat is self-mutilating — biting at the skin aggressively.
- Itching is so severe the cat cannot sleep or eat.

Routine visit (within 1–2 weeks) for:
- Any persistent hair loss that does not resolve in 2–3 weeks.
- Recurrent ear infections alongside skin issues.
- Any scaling, crusting, or skin thickening that is worsening.

## Prevention
section: prevention

- Maintain strict monthly flea prevention for all pets in the household.
- Feed a high-quality, species-appropriate diet — skin health reflects nutritional status.
- Supplement with omega-3 fatty acids (EPA and DHA from fish oil) — these have well-documented anti-inflammatory effects on the skin.
- Minimise stress — multiple hiding spots, vertical space, and predictable routines reduce anxiety-related over-grooming.
- Quarantine new cats and screen for ringworm before introducing to the household.
