# Feline Anorexia and Loss of Appetite

## Overview
section: overview

A cat that refuses to eat for more than 24 hours is always a concern. Cats are obligate carnivores with a high protein metabolism, and unlike dogs or humans, they cannot safely fast for extended periods. Even 48–72 hours without food can lead to a dangerous condition called hepatic lipidosis (fatty liver disease), where the liver is forced to process body fat for energy, overwhelming hepatocytes. Loss of appetite (anorexia) in cats is a symptom rather than a disease itself — it almost always signals an underlying problem that needs attention.

## Common Causes
section: causes

The most frequent causes of reduced or absent appetite in cats include:
- **Respiratory infections (URI):** Cats rely heavily on smell to trigger appetite. A blocked nose means they cannot smell food, so they stop eating.
- **Dental pain:** Tooth resorption, gingivitis, or oral ulcers make eating painful. Look for drooling, pawing at the mouth, or only eating on one side.
- **Gastrointestinal issues:** Nausea from hairballs, constipation, inflammatory bowel disease, or foreign-body obstruction.
- **Kidney disease (CKD):** Toxin build-up causes persistent nausea and appetite suppression in cats with chronic kidney disease.
- **Stress or environmental change:** A new pet, a new home, a change in food brand, or even moving the food bowl can cause a cat to stop eating.
- **Pancreatitis:** Inflammation of the pancreas, often alongside vomiting and lethargy.
- **Hyperthyroidism (in seniors):** Paradoxically, some hyperthyroid cats eat voraciously while others lose appetite and weight rapidly.
- **Cancer or systemic illness:** Sustained anorexia with weight loss in older cats always warrants thorough veterinary investigation.

## Home Assessment
section: assessment

Before visiting a vet, assess the following at home:
1. When did the cat last eat, and how much?
2. Has anything changed — food brand, bowl position, household routine, new pet or person?
3. Is the cat drinking water? Dehydration alongside anorexia is urgent.
4. Is the cat vomiting, having diarrhea, or straining to urinate or defecate?
5. Does the cat seem painful when you touch the abdomen?
6. Are there any signs of respiratory infection — sneezing, runny nose?

## Home Care Tips
section: treatment

If the cat missed only one meal and seems otherwise bright:
- Warm wet food slightly (to body temperature, about 38°C) to enhance aroma.
- Try a different protein — if your cat usually eats chicken, offer tuna or salmon.
- Offer small amounts frequently (every 2–3 hours) rather than leaving food out.
- Add a small amount of low-sodium chicken broth to the food.
- Remove any stressors — separate the cat from other pets, provide a quiet, private feeding space.
- Do NOT force-feed or syringe large amounts of food, as aspiration is dangerous.

## When to See a Vet Immediately
section: emergency

Take your cat to the vet urgently if:
- The cat has not eaten for more than 24–36 hours.
- The cat is also not drinking and appears lethargic.
- You notice yellowing of the skin, gums, or whites of the eyes (jaundice — a sign of hepatic lipidosis).
- The cat is vomiting repeatedly or has blood in vomit.
- The cat appears to be in pain — crying, hunched posture, hiding.
- The cat is a kitten under 6 months or a senior over 10 years — they decompensate faster.
- Weight loss has been ongoing for several weeks.

## Prevention
section: prevention

- Feed a consistent, high-quality diet appropriate for the cat's age and health status.
- Maintain a predictable feeding schedule and environment.
- Schedule annual vet check-ups to catch underlying disease early.
- Transition food brands gradually over 7–10 days to avoid GI upset.
- Keep dental health in check with regular brushing or dental treats.
