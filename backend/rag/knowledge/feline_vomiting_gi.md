## Overview
section: overview

Vomiting is one of the most common reasons cat owners seek veterinary care. A single episode of vomiting in an otherwise healthy cat is usually not serious, but repeated vomiting — especially when accompanied by lethargy, blood, or anorexia — is always a red flag. It is important to distinguish between vomiting (active abdominal contractions, forceful expulsion) and regurgitation (passive, effortless expulsion from the oesophagus, which happens shortly after eating). The distinction matters because they have very different causes and treatments.

---

## Common Causes
section: causes

**Acute (sudden onset) vomiting:**
- Dietary indiscretion — eating too fast, eating grass, eating something unsuitable
- Hairballs (trichobezoars) — most common in long-haired breeds; the cat retches repeatedly before producing a cigar-shaped wad of fur
- Food intolerance or sudden diet change
- Intestinal parasites (roundworms, tapeworms)
- Bacterial or viral gastroenteritis
- Foreign body obstruction — string, rubber bands, small toys are extremely dangerous
- Toxin ingestion — lilies, onions, ibuprofen, chocolate are all highly toxic to cats

**Chronic (ongoing or recurrent) vomiting:**
- Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) — the most common cause of chronic vomiting in cats
- Hyperthyroidism — excess thyroid hormone increases gut motility and causes chronic nausea
- Chronic kidney disease — uraemic toxins trigger the vomiting centre in the brain
- Pancreatitis — often triad of pancreatitis + IBD + cholangitis ("triaditis")
- Intestinal lymphoma — a cancer of the GI tract that closely mimics IBD in older cats
- Diabetes mellitus — vomiting alongside increased thirst and weight loss

## Hairball vs. Vomiting
section: assessment

Hairballs are technically a form of vomiting but warrant their own assessment:
- **Hairball:** The cat gags and retches for 30–60 seconds and then produces a cylindrical, tube-shaped mass of fur mixed with bile. The cat immediately returns to normal behaviour.
- **True vomiting:** The cat shows nausea (lip-licking, drooling, looking uncomfortable before the event), brings up partially digested food or bile, and may continue to look unwell afterwards.
- **Regurgitation:** Food comes up immediately after eating with no retching, appears undigested, tubular in shape — often a sign of oesophageal disease (megaoesophagus, stricture).

## Home Care (Mild, Isolated Vomiting)
section: treatment

For a single episode of vomiting in an otherwise bright cat:
1. Withhold food for 2–4 hours to let the stomach settle, but always keep water available.
2. After 2–4 hours, offer a small amount of bland food: plain boiled chicken (no seasoning) or a prescription GI diet.
3. Feed small amounts every 3–4 hours and gradually return to normal food over 24–48 hours.
4. For hairball prevention: use a hairball lubricant gel (malt paste), brush the cat regularly to remove loose fur, and consider a hairball-control diet.
5. Monitor closely — if vomiting recurs or the cat deteriorates, seek veterinary care.

## When to See a Vet Immediately
section: emergency

Rush to a vet if:
- Vomiting is frequent — more than 3 times in a 24-hour period.
- There is blood in the vomit (bright red or coffee-ground appearance — the latter indicates digested blood).
- The cat is also refusing water and appears dehydrated (dry gums, skin pinch stays tented).
- The abdomen appears bloated, hard, or the cat cries when you touch it.
- The cat has eaten a known toxin, string, or small object.
- The cat is a kitten under 12 weeks — they dehydrate and crash extremely rapidly.
- Vomiting is accompanied by yellow-green bile every 30 minutes (may indicate obstruction).
- The cat is also not urinating — combined with vomiting, this is a urinary emergency.

## Diagnosis and Treatment
section: treatment

A vet will typically perform:
- **Blood panel:** To assess kidney function, liver enzymes, blood glucose, and thyroid levels.
- **Urinalysis:** Especially if kidney disease or diabetes is suspected.
- **Abdominal X-ray:** To check for foreign bodies, obstruction, or gas patterns.
- **Abdominal ultrasound:** The gold standard for evaluating intestinal wall thickness (IBD vs. lymphoma) and the pancreas.
- **Biopsy (endoscopy or surgical):** Required to definitively distinguish IBD from intestinal lymphoma.

Treatments vary from a simple bland diet change, to antiparasitic medication, to long-term immunosuppressive therapy for IBD, to chemotherapy (typically chlorambucil + prednisolone) for low-grade GI lymphoma.

## Prevention
section: prevention

- Feed a high-quality, age-appropriate diet and avoid sudden food changes.
- Keep toxic plants (especially lilies), medications, and small objects out of reach.
- Groom long-haired cats regularly and provide hairball remedies.
- Keep up with routine deworming and parasite prevention.
- Schedule annual blood panels for cats over 7 years old to catch early organ disease.
