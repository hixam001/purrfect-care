## Overview
section: overview

Feline Lower Urinary Tract Disease (FLUTD) is an umbrella term for a group of conditions affecting the bladder and urethra in cats. The most common form is Feline Idiopathic Cystitis (FIC), which accounts for around 60–70% of all FLUTD cases and has no identifiable anatomical or infectious cause — it is strongly linked to stress. Other causes include urolithiasis (bladder stones), urethral plugs, bacterial infection, and anatomical defects. FLUTD is life-threatening in male cats when it causes a urethral blockage ("blocked cat") — one of the most common veterinary emergencies.

---

## Signs of FLUTD
section: assessment

All forms of FLUTD share similar signs because they all irritate the bladder or urethra:
- Straining to urinate (dysuria) — the cat squats in or outside the litter box for prolonged periods, often producing little or no urine
- Frequent, short visits to the litter box (pollakiuria)
- Blood in the urine (haematuria) — the urine may appear pink or red
- Urinating in unusual places (periuria) — the floor, the bath, the sink — often on cool surfaces because the cat associates the litter box with pain
- Excessive licking at the genital area
- Crying or vocalising while trying to urinate
- In male cats especially: crying in pain, lethargy, vomiting, complete inability to produce urine

## Urethral Blockage — A Life-Threatening Emergency
section: emergency

**This is the most important thing to know about FLUTD:**

A urethral obstruction (blocked bladder) in a male cat is a life-threatening emergency. Male cats have a very narrow urethra that can become blocked by urethral plugs (mucus, protein, crystals), stones, or spasm.

**A blocked male cat will die within 24–48 hours if untreated** from potassium toxicity causing cardiac arrest, or from bladder rupture.

**Emergency signs — call your vet immediately:**
- A male cat that has been straining to urinate for more than 2–4 hours without producing urine
- A male cat that is crying, vomiting, or completely lethargic combined with straining
- A hard, enlarged, painful bladder you can feel in the lower abdomen (like a firm ball)
- Any male cat that suddenly collapses or becomes unresponsive — this is cardiac arrest from hyperkalaemia

**Female cats can also develop blockages but it is much less common due to their wider, shorter urethra.**

## Feline Idiopathic Cystitis (FIC) — Stress Cystitis
section: causes

The most common cause of FLUTD in cats under 10 years. The bladder wall becomes inflamed in response to stress hormones, causing all the signs of a urinary infection despite normal urine culture. Key features:
- Strongly stress-linked: episodes are triggered by changes in routine, new pets, multi-cat conflict, house moves, changes in food
- Self-limiting: most episodes resolve within 5–7 days with supportive care
- Recurrent: cats with FIC tend to have repeat episodes throughout their lives
- Diagnosis of exclusion: a vet must rule out infection, stones, and blockage first

**Management of FIC:**
- Environmental stress reduction is the cornerstone of treatment
- Increase water intake: switch to wet food, provide a water fountain, add water to food
- Increase litter boxes: minimum one per cat plus one extra; clean daily
- Provide hiding spots, vertical space, and separate resource areas in multi-cat homes
- Feliway (synthetic feline facial pheromone) diffusers reduce stress-related FIC episodes
- Prescription urinary diets (Royal Canin Calm, Hill's c/d Stress) can reduce recurrence
- Amitriptyline or gabapentin for cats with very frequent recurrences

## Bladder Stones (Urolithiasis)
section: causes

Crystals in the urine can coalesce into bladder stones. The two most common types in cats are:

**Struvite (magnesium ammonium phosphate):**
- Form in alkaline urine, often secondary to bacterial infection
- More common in females
- Often dissolve with a prescription dissolution diet (Royal Canin Urinary SO, Hill's s/d)

**Calcium oxalate:**
- Form in acidic urine; associated with high calcium, low moisture diets
- More common in older male cats and certain breeds (Burmese, Persian, Himalayan)
- Cannot be dissolved with diet — require surgical removal (cystotomy) or laser lithotripsy
- Prevention: high-moisture diet, increased water intake, urine pH monitoring

## Bacterial Urinary Tract Infection (UTI)
section: causes

True bacterial UTI is actually uncommon in young cats with FLUTD (accounts for only about 1–2% of cases in young cats). It becomes more common in senior cats, diabetic cats, cats on long-term steroids, and cats with CKD. Diagnosis requires a urine culture and sensitivity (C&S) — not just a dipstick test. Treatment: a 7–14 day course of the appropriate antibiotic based on culture results. Avoid empirical antibiotics without culture — antimicrobial resistance is a growing concern.

## Treatment Overview
section: treatment

- **Blocked cat:** Emergency urethral catheterisation under anaesthesia, IV fluids, monitoring of potassium and kidney values, and management of the underlying cause.
- **FIC (unblocked):** Pain relief (buprenorphine), antispasmodics (prazosin), stress reduction, increased water intake, possible prescription urinary diet.
- **Struvite stones:** Dissolution diet for 4–12 weeks with monthly monitoring X-rays.
- **Calcium oxalate stones:** Surgical cystotomy.
- **Bacterial UTI:** Culture-guided antibiotics.

## Prevention
section: prevention

- Feed primarily wet food — the most evidence-based intervention for FLUTD prevention. Wet food doubles urinary water intake, dilutes the urine, and reduces crystal formation.
- Provide a water fountain — many cats prefer moving water and drink significantly more.
- Maintain at least one litter box per cat plus one extra; scoop at least once daily.
- Reduce environmental stress — predictable routines, plenty of hiding spots, vertical space, and resource separation in multi-cat homes.
- Monitor your cat for subtle signs of urinary discomfort — early detection prevents blockage.
- Annual urinalysis for cats over 7 years to detect early crystal formation.
