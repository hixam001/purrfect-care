## Overview
section: overview

Intestinal parasites are extremely common in cats, particularly in kittens and outdoor or rescue cats. Many infected cats show no obvious signs at all, which is why routine deworming and faecal testing are standard preventive care. The most important intestinal parasites of cats are roundworms (Toxocara cati), tapeworms (Dipylidium caninum and Taenia species), hookworms (Ancylostoma tubaeforme), and the protozoal parasites Giardia and Toxoplasma gondii. Some of these are zoonotic — meaning they can be transmitted to humans — making proper hygiene and treatment important for the whole family.

---

## Roundworms (Toxocara cati)
section: causes

Roundworms are the most common intestinal parasite in cats worldwide. Adult worms are large (up to 15 cm), cream-coloured, and look like spaghetti. They live in the small intestine and steal nutrients from the host.

**Transmission:**
- Ingestion of eggs from contaminated soil, water, or faeces (common in kittens)
- Hunting and eating infected rodents or birds (paratenic hosts)
- Transmission from queen to kittens via milk (transmammary transmission)

**Signs (especially in kittens):**
- Pot-bellied, distended abdomen
- Failure to thrive, poor growth, weight loss
- Dull coat and lethargy
- Vomiting (may vomit whole worms — alarming but not immediately life-threatening)
- Visible worms in faeces (look like moving spaghetti strands)
- Diarrhoea or intermittent soft stools
- Heavy burdens in kittens can cause intestinal obstruction — a surgical emergency

**Zoonotic risk:** Toxocara eggs in soil can accidentally infect humans, particularly children, causing visceral larva migrans — a serious condition where larvae migrate through organs. Always wash hands after handling cats and their litter boxes.

## Tapeworms (Dipylidium caninum and Taenia spp.)
section: causes

Tapeworms are segmented flat worms that attach to the intestinal wall. The body is made up of hundreds of segments (proglottids) each containing eggs.

**Dipylidium caninum — the flea tapeworm:**
The most common tapeworm in cats. Cats get it by swallowing an infected flea (during grooming). If your cat has tapeworms, they almost certainly also have fleas.

**Signs of tapeworm:**
- Proglottids (egg-filled segments) visible on the fur around the anus or in faeces — they look like grains of white or cream-coloured rice. Fresh segments may move.
- Scooting or excessive licking of the anal area (irritation from segments emerging)
- Most cats show no other signs; heavy infestations cause weight loss and dull coat

**Treatment:** Praziquantel (oral or injectable). Crucially, you must also treat the cat (and the home environment) for fleas, or the cat will rapidly become re-infected.

## Hookworms (Ancylostoma tubaeforme)
section: causes

Less common than roundworms and tapeworms in cats but more pathogenic. Adult hookworms attach to the intestinal mucosa and actively suck blood.

**Signs:**
- Anaemia (pale gums, weakness, lethargy) — especially dangerous in kittens
- Tarry, black stools (digested blood)
- Bloody diarrhoea in severe cases
- Weight loss

**Treatment:** Pyrantel pamoate, fenbendazole, milbemycin oxime, or moxidectin.

## Giardia
section: causes

Giardia is a single-celled protozoan (not a worm) that colonises the small intestine. It is common in kittens and cats from shelters or multi-cat environments.

**Signs:**
- Soft, pale, greasy-looking diarrhoea that may be intermittent
- Diarrhoea that does not respond to routine dietary management
- Weight loss in chronic cases
- Many cats are asymptomatic carriers

**Diagnosis:** Faecal ELISA antigen test or PCR — routine faecal flotation often misses Giardia.
**Treatment:** Fenbendazole (5 days) or metronidazole. Bathing the cat during treatment removes cysts from the coat that the cat would otherwise re-ingest during grooming.
**Zoonotic risk:** Some Giardia genotypes can infect humans; thorough hand washing is essential.

---

## Toxoplasma gondii
section: causes

Toxoplasma is a protozoan for which cats are the definitive host — meaning only cats (and other felids) can complete the sexual reproductive cycle and shed infective oocysts in faeces.

**In cats:** Most healthy adult cats are asymptomatic. Kittens or immunocompromised cats may develop respiratory, neurological, or eye signs.
**Shedding:** Cats only shed oocysts for 1–3 weeks after their first infection. Oocysts take 1–5 days after passing to become infective — scooping the litter box daily prevents human exposure.
**Zoonotic risk:** Critical for pregnant women and immunocompromised individuals. Pregnant women should ideally not handle litter boxes, or should wear gloves and wash hands thoroughly.

## Diagnosis
section: assessment

A faecal examination is essential for diagnosis:
- **Faecal flotation:** Detects most roundworm and hookworm eggs. Less reliable for Giardia.
- **Faecal antigen test (ELISA):** The most sensitive test for Giardia.
- **Faecal PCR panel:** The gold standard; detects eggs, protozoa, and viral pathogens simultaneously.

Always bring a fresh faecal sample (collected within 12 hours, stored in the fridge) to your vet visit.

## When to See a Vet
section: emergency

See a vet urgently if:
- A kitten appears pot-bellied, fails to gain weight, or vomits worms.
- Any cat has pale or white gums (sign of anaemia from hookworms or heavy roundworm burden).
- Bloody, black, or tarry stools are present.
- A kitten has severe, frequent diarrhoea — dehydration develops rapidly.

Routine visit:
- Any newly acquired kitten should be dewormed immediately and again at 2-week intervals until 12 weeks, then monthly until 6 months.
- Faecal testing for all new cats before introducing to a multi-cat household.

## Prevention and Deworming Schedule
section: prevention

- **Kittens:** Deworm at 2, 4, 6, and 8 weeks of age, then monthly until 6 months.
- **Adult indoor cats:** Faecal test every 6–12 months; deworm if positive or as your vet recommends.
- **Outdoor/hunting cats:** Deworm every 1–3 months due to ongoing exposure from prey.
- **Flea control:** Essential to prevent tapeworm re-infection — use a monthly topical or oral flea preventative.
- **Litter hygiene:** Scoop daily, fully disinfect with boiling water or dilute bleach monthly.
- **Handwashing:** Always wash hands after handling cats, their waste, or gardening in soil where cats may defecate.
