## Overview
section: overview

Diarrhea in cats — loose, watery, or more frequent stools — is one of the most common gastrointestinal complaints. It is a symptom rather than a disease and can arise from a huge range of causes, from mild dietary indiscretion to life-threatening conditions. The clinical approach differs significantly depending on whether the diarrhea originates from the small intestine or large intestine, and whether the onset was acute (sudden, less than 2 weeks) or chronic (persistent for more than 3 weeks). Cats can deteriorate rapidly when diarrhea is combined with vomiting, especially kittens, so early assessment is critical.

## Small Intestinal vs. Large Intestinal Diarrhea
section: assessment

Distinguishing the source of diarrhea guides diagnosis significantly:

**Small intestinal diarrhea:**
- Large volume of watery stool
- Produced 2–4 times per day (normal frequency)
- May contain digested (dark) blood
- Weight loss is common in chronic cases
- Urgency is less prominent
- Common causes: parasites (roundworms, Giardia), dietary indiscretion, IBD, lymphoma, food allergy, pancreatitis, infectious enteritis

**Large intestinal diarrhea (colitis):**
- Small volumes of soft or liquid stool produced very frequently (5–15+ times per day)
- Obvious straining and urgency — may look like the cat is constipated when actually they have colitis
- Fresh (bright red) blood and mucus in the stool is very common
- Weight loss is unusual
- Common causes: stress colitis, dietary intolerance, Tritrichomonas foetus (in cats from catteries), IBD affecting the colon, polyps or masses

## Acute Diarrhea — Common Causes
section: causes

**Dietary indiscretion:** Sudden food change, eating spoiled food, eating prey, eating inappropriate human food. The most common cause of a single episode of diarrhea.

**Infectious causes:**
- **Feline panleukopenia (FPV/feline parvovirus):** Severe, often bloody diarrhea with vomiting in unvaccinated cats; very high mortality in kittens; highly contagious
- **Salmonella, Campylobacter:** From raw food diets or hunting
- **Giardia:** Pale, greasy soft stools, intermittent, often without blood; common in young cats from shelters
- **Tritrichomonas foetus:** Chronic large-intestinal diarrhea in catteries; particularly common in Bengals and purebred cats; requires specific testing (PCR) and treatment (ronidazole)
- **Cryptosporidium:** Intermittent diarrhea in young or immunocompromised cats; diagnosed by PCR

**Stress colitis:** A single stressful event (vet visit, thunderstorm, new pet) can trigger 1–5 days of colitis with urgency and fresh blood. Usually self-limiting.

**Medication side effects:** Antibiotics, NSAIDs, certain dewormers.

**Toxin ingestion:** Spoiled food, toxic plants, household chemicals.

## Chronic Diarrhea — Common Causes
section: causes

**Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD):**
The most common cause of chronic diarrhea in adult cats. An immune-mediated condition causing chronic infiltration of inflammatory cells into the intestinal wall, impairing absorption and motility. The triad of IBD + pancreatitis + cholangitis (liver inflammation) — called "triaditis" — is common in cats. IBD can affect the small intestine (causing weight loss and vomiting predominantly) or the colon (causing chronic large-bowel diarrhea). Treatment: hypoallergenic or novel-protein diet trials, B12 (cobalamin) supplementation (often deficient), prednisolone, and sometimes chlorambucil.

**Intestinal Lymphoma (Small Cell):**
Mimics IBD clinically and histologically — they can only be reliably distinguished by biopsy (full-thickness surgical biopsy or endoscopic biopsy). Fortunately, the most common form of feline GI lymphoma (small-cell/low-grade lymphoma) responds extremely well to treatment with chlorambucil and prednisolone, with median survival times of 2–3 years.

**Exocrine Pancreatic Insufficiency (EPI):**
Rare in cats but causes profuse, fatty, voluminous diarrhea and dramatic weight loss. The pancreas fails to produce digestive enzymes. Treatment: pancreatic enzyme powder supplementation with every meal.

**Food allergy/intolerance:** As described in the skin allergies document — chronic diarrhea and intermittent vomiting alongside skin signs.

**Hyperthyroidism:** Increased gut motility causes chronic soft stools or diarrhea, especially in senior cats.

## Assessment at Home
section: assessment

Collect the following information before your vet visit:
1. **Duration:** When did it start?
2. **Frequency:** How many times per day?
3. **Consistency:** Soft, liquid, frothy, fatty/greasy?
4. **Blood or mucus:** Bright red blood? Dark/digested blood? Clear or bloody mucus?
5. **Vomiting:** Is the cat also vomiting? Combined diarrhea and vomiting dehydrates rapidly.
6. **Appetite and drinking:** Normal? Reduced? Absent?
7. **Weight:** Is the cat losing weight?
8. **Recent changes:** New food, new medication, recent stressors, access to prey or outdoors?
9. **Other pets:** Do any other pets in the household have the same signs? (Important for infectious causes)

If possible, collect a fresh faecal sample (within 12 hours, in a clean container, refrigerated if not going immediately) to bring to the vet.

## Home Care (Mild, Acute Diarrhea in an Otherwise Well Cat)
section: treatment

For a single episode of loose stool in a bright, eating, non-vomiting adult cat:
1. Withhold food for 4–6 hours but always keep fresh water available.
2. Offer a small amount of bland food: plain boiled chicken or white fish (unseasoned), or a prescription GI diet (Hill's i/d, Royal Canin Gastrointestinal).
3. Feed small amounts 3–4 times per day and gradually reintroduce normal diet over 3–4 days.
4. Add a probiotic formulated for cats (e.g., FortiFlora, Visbiome Vet) — proven to reduce duration of acute diarrhea.
5. Monitor hydration: gently pinch the skin at the scruff — it should spring back immediately. Slow or no return to position indicates dehydration.
6. If diarrhea persists beyond 48 hours or worsens, see a vet.

## When to See a Vet
section: emergency

Seek emergency veterinary care immediately if:
- Large amounts of bright red blood are present in the stool.
- Diarrhea is black and tarry (melena — digested blood from upper GI bleeding or ingested toxin).
- The cat is also vomiting repeatedly and appears dehydrated.
- A kitten under 16 weeks is affected — kittens can become critically dehydrated within hours.
- The cat is unvaccinated and lives with other cats — suspect panleukopenia.
- The cat appears lethargic, has pale gums, or is cold to the touch.
- The cat is straining constantly and producing only small amounts of blood and mucus — may be severe colitis or, in male cats, a urinary emergency.

See a vet within 24–48 hours if:
- Diarrhea has lasted more than 48 hours despite dietary management.
- Weight loss is occurring alongside chronic diarrhea.
- The cat is very young (under 6 months), senior (over 10 years), or immunocompromised.

## Diagnostic Tests
section: treatment

- **Faecal PCR panel:** The most sensitive and comprehensive test — detects bacterial pathogens, viral causes (panleukopenia, coronavirus), and protozoal parasites (Giardia, Tritrichomonas, Cryptosporidium) simultaneously.
- **Faecal flotation and direct smear:** Basic screening for helminth eggs and motile protozoa.
- **Blood panel:** Biochemistry (kidney, liver, total protein, glucose), CBC, B12 (cobalamin), folate, TLI (trypsin-like immunoreactivity — tests for EPI and pancreatitis).
- **Spec fPL (feline-specific pancreatic lipase):** Tests for pancreatitis.
- **Abdominal ultrasound:** Assesses intestinal wall layering, lymph node enlargement, and pancreas.
- **Biopsy:** Endoscopic or full-thickness surgical biopsy is required to definitively diagnose IBD vs. lymphoma.

## Prevention
section: prevention

- Feed a consistent, high-quality diet — avoid frequent food changes.
- Transition new foods gradually over 7–10 days.
- Keep up with routine deworming and vaccination (especially panleukopenia).
- Quarantine new cats before introducing to a multi-cat household (Tritrichomonas and Giardia are shed in stool and spread easily).
- Do not feed raw meat diets without veterinary guidance — Salmonella and Campylobacter contamination is common.
- Manage stress proactively — Feliway, environmental enrichment, and predictable routines reduce stress colitis.
