## Overview
section: overview

A cat that is drinking noticeably more water than usual is displaying a symptom called polydipsia. Normal water intake for a cat is approximately 50–60 ml per kilogram of body weight per day (a 4 kg cat should drink around 200–240 ml per day). Cats that eat primarily wet food naturally drink less because their food provides most of their water. Increased drinking is almost always accompanied by increased urination (polyuria). Together, PU/PD (polyuria/polydipsia) is one of the most important diagnostic signals in feline medicine, because it is a hallmark of several serious but treatable diseases.

---

## Major Causes
section: causes

The four most common causes of PU/PD in cats are:

**1. Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD):**
The most common cause in middle-aged to older cats (typically over 7 years). As kidneys lose their concentrating ability, the cat must drink more to compensate for dilute urine. CKD is progressive and incurable but manageable with diet, fluids, and medication.

**2. Diabetes Mellitus:**
High blood glucose spills into the urine (glucosuria), pulling water with it through osmotic diuresis. Affected cats drink and urinate heavily, may have a ragged coat, and often walk with a plantigrade (flat-footed) stance due to diabetic neuropathy. Many cats achieve diabetic remission with early treatment and a low-carbohydrate diet.

**3. Hyperthyroidism:**
The most common hormonal disorder in cats over 10 years old. Excess thyroid hormone increases metabolism and blood pressure, which damages the kidneys over time and drives PU/PD. Affected cats often eat a lot but lose weight. A palpable thyroid nodule in the neck is a classic finding.

**4. Pyometra (intact females):**
An infected, pus-filled uterus in an unspayed female cat. Bacterial toxins interfere with kidney tubule function, causing PU/PD. This is a life-threatening emergency.

Less common causes: liver disease, hypercalcaemia, hypokalaemia, Cushing's disease (rare in cats), psychogenic polydipsia, certain medications (e.g., steroids, diuretics).

## Assessment at Home
section: assessment

You can measure your cat's water intake over 24 hours:
1. Fill a measuring jug with a known volume of water (e.g., 500 ml).
2. Use only that jug to fill the water bowl for 24 hours.
3. At the end of 24 hours, measure how much water is left.
4. Account for any water that spilled.
5. If your cat consumed more than 100 ml/kg/day, that is polydipsia and warrants a vet visit.

Also observe:
- Is the litter box more wet than usual? Large clumps of urine-soaked litter?
- Is the cat urinating outside the litter box (suggesting urgency)?
- Is the cat losing weight despite eating normally or more than usual?
- Does the cat appear lethargic, have a poor coat, or vomit occasionally?

## When to See a Vet
section: emergency

See a vet soon (within 1–3 days) if:
- Your cat is drinking noticeably more than usual and you've ruled out heat or a dry-food diet change.
- Weight loss is occurring alongside increased drinking.
- The cat also vomits occasionally or has a poor, unkempt coat.

See a vet urgently (same day) if:
- The cat is an intact (unspayed) female — pyometra is life-threatening.
- The cat appears very lethargic, depressed, or is not eating.
- The cat is also straining to urinate or not producing urine at all.
- You have diabetic cats who are vomiting — may be in diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), an emergency.

## Diagnosis
section: treatment

Blood and urine tests are essential. A vet will typically run:
- **Biochemistry panel:** BUN, creatinine, SDMA (kidney markers); glucose (diabetes); liver enzymes; total protein.
- **Complete blood count (CBC):** Anaemia (common in CKD), infection markers.
- **Total T4:** Thyroid hormone level — the single most important test in cats over 8 years.
- **Urinalysis:** Specific gravity (is the urine concentrated?), glucose, protein, sediment.
- **Blood pressure:** Hypertension is common with CKD and hyperthyroidism and can cause sudden blindness.

---

## Management Overview
section: treatment

- **CKD:** Prescription renal diet (low phosphorus, controlled protein), phosphate binders, blood pressure medication (amlodipine), subcutaneous fluids at home.
- **Diabetes:** Low-carbohydrate diet (high-protein wet food), insulin injections (typically glargine or PZI), blood glucose monitoring. Up to 80% of cats achieve remission with aggressive early management.
- **Hyperthyroidism:** Methimazole (medication), radioactive iodine therapy (I-131, curative), prescription iodine-restricted diet (Hill's y/d), or surgical thyroidectomy.
- **Pyometra:** Emergency ovariohysterectomy (spay) with IV fluids and antibiotics.

---

## Prevention
section: prevention

- Spay female cats to prevent pyometra.
- Feed a species-appropriate diet (high protein, moderate fat, low carbohydrate wet food).
- Provide ample fresh water — many cats prefer running water (a pet fountain encourages drinking).
- Schedule annual blood and urine panels for cats over 7 years old.
- Monitor weight and body condition score monthly at home.
