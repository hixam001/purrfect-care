# Feline Lameness and Limping

## Overview
section: overview

A limping cat is always a concern. Cats are stoic animals that instinctively mask pain — if a cat is limping visibly, the pain is likely significant. Lameness (limping) can affect any leg and results from pain, structural abnormality, or neurological dysfunction anywhere from the toes to the spine. The approach differs depending on whether the onset was sudden (acute) or gradual (chronic), and whether it affects one limb or multiple limbs. Never give a cat human pain medications — paracetamol (acetaminophen) is rapidly fatal in cats, and ibuprofen (NSAIDS) and aspirin are severely toxic.

---

## Common Causes by Age Group
section: causes

**Kittens and young cats (under 2 years):**
- Trauma: fractures, dislocations, and soft tissue injuries from falls or road traffic accidents
- Infectious polyarthritis: joint infection secondary to cat bite wounds
- Osteochondrosis (OCD): developmental cartilage problem in rapidly growing cats
- Nutritional secondary hyperparathyroidism: bone weakness from an all-meat diet deficient in calcium

**Adult cats (2–8 years):**
- Trauma remains the most common cause at any age
- Paw pad injuries: cuts, burns, thorns, or foreign bodies embedded between toes
- Nail injuries: broken, torn, or overgrown nails curled into the paw pad
- Cat bite abscess: a painful swelling at the site of an infected bite wound
- Thromboembolism: a blood clot (usually from heart disease) lodging in the arteries supplying the hind limbs — sudden, severe, paralysis of one or both hind legs with cold, painful limbs and vocalisation

**Senior cats (over 8 years):**
- Degenerative joint disease (osteoarthritis / DJD): the most under-diagnosed condition in senior cats; affects over 60% of cats over 6 years and 90% over 12 years
- Bone cancer (osteosarcoma): rapidly progressive, painful swelling of a bone
- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) with thromboembolism: a common cause of sudden hind-limb paralysis

## Assessing a Limping Cat at Home
section: assessment

Gently examine the affected limb from toe to shoulder or hip:
1. **Paws first:** Look between the toes for cuts, swelling, embedded foreign bodies, or broken/overgrown nails. Gently squeeze each toe — does the cat react or pull away?
2. **Pads:** Check for burns, cuts, blisters, or thorns. In summer, hot pavement burns are common.
3. **Joints:** Feel along the leg for swelling, heat, or deformity.
4. **Spine/pelvis:** If the cat is dragging both hind legs or has sudden inability to use them, this is an emergency — do not manipulate the spine.
5. **Temperament:** A limping cat that is hissing, growling, or vocalising is in significant pain — minimise handling and go to a vet.

## Immediate Home First Aid
section: treatment

- Confine the cat to a small, warm, quiet area — restrict jumping and stair-climbing.
- If you can see a wound, clean it gently with saline or cooled boiled water and a clean cloth. Do NOT use hydrogen peroxide or alcohol — these damage tissue.
- If a bone is visibly deformed or protruding, do not attempt to straighten it. Wrap the cat loosely in a blanket for warmth and transport to a vet immediately.
- **Do not give any pain medication** — paracetamol (acetaminophen) is fatal in cats. Ibuprofen and aspirin are also dangerous. Only use vet-prescribed feline NSAIDs (meloxicam) or opioids.

## When to See a Vet Immediately
section: emergency

This is an emergency if:
- The cat suddenly cannot use one or both hind legs, the limbs feel cold, and the cat is vocalising in pain — this pattern (aortic thromboembolism / "saddle thrombus") requires emergency treatment within hours.
- Any bone is visibly displaced, fractured, or an open wound is present.
- The cat was hit by a car or fell from a significant height — even if walking, internal injuries may be present.
- The paw is cold, blue/purple, or has no sensation.
- The cat appears to be in severe pain, is trembling, or is unable to stand at all.
- Swelling is rapidly increasing, or there is a hot, fluctuant lump (abscess) developing.

See a vet within 24 hours if:
- Mild lameness persists for more than 24 hours without improvement.
- There is visible swelling or heat around a joint.
- The cat is licking one leg obsessively (often indicates a wound or embedded foreign body).

## Treatment Options
section: treatment

- **Trauma / fractures:** Splinting, casting, surgical repair with plates and screws, or amputation in severe cases. Cats recover remarkably well on three legs.
- **Abscesses:** Lancing, flushing, and a course of antibiotics (amoxicillin-clavulanate or clindamycin).
- **Osteoarthritis:** Weight management, joint supplements (omega-3 fatty acids, glucosamine), environmental modifications (ramps, heated beds), and feline-approved pain relief (meloxicam, gabapentin, monthly monoclonal antibody injection: frunevetmab/Solensia).
- **Thromboembolism:** Emergency anti-thrombotic therapy (heparin, clopidogrel), pain management, and treatment of the underlying heart disease.

## Prevention
section: prevention

- Keep cats indoors to prevent road accidents, fights, and falls.
- Trim nails every 3–4 weeks to prevent overgrowth and ingrowing.
- Maintain a healthy weight — obesity dramatically worsens osteoarthritis.
- Provide ramps and steps to elevated resting spots for senior cats.
- Screen senior cats annually for heart disease (echocardiogram).
