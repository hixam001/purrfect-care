## Overview
section: overview

Eye problems are common in cats and range from mild, self-limiting conjunctivitis to emergencies that can cause permanent vision loss within hours if untreated. The eye is a vulnerable, exquisitely sensitive organ — any persistent eye abnormality warrants prompt veterinary attention. Cats are generally stoic but will often squint, paw at the eye, or hold it closed when it hurts. Never apply human eye drops, especially those containing steroids or vasoconstrictors, to a cat's eye without veterinary guidance — steroids are contraindicated if a corneal ulcer is present and can cause rapid, catastrophic deterioration.

## Normal Eye Anatomy and Discharge
section: assessment

A small amount of clear or slightly rusty-coloured discharge in the inner corner of the eye is normal in most cats. This is dried tear residue (porphyrins) and is especially noticeable in light-coloured cats.

**Abnormal findings include:**
- Continuous watery or excessive tearing (epiphora)
- Yellow, green, or thick grey-white discharge — indicates infection (bacterial)
- One eye clearly smaller, more closed, or sunken than the other
- Redness of the white of the eye (sclera) or the inner lining (conjunctiva)
- Cloudiness, bluish haze, or white spots on the normally clear cornea
- The third eyelid (nictitating membrane) visible and partially covering the eye — often a sign of illness, pain, or dehydration
- Pawing at the eye or rubbing the face on surfaces
- Squinting or keeping one eye tightly closed (blepharospasm)
- Asymmetric pupils (one larger than the other — anisocoria)

## Conjunctivitis (Cat Eye Infection)
section: causes

Conjunctivitis — inflammation of the conjunctiva (the pink tissue lining the eyelids) — is the most common eye problem in cats. It causes redness, discharge, and squinting.

**Causes:**
- **Feline Herpesvirus-1 (FHV-1):** The most common cause of conjunctivitis in cats. Most cats are infected as kittens during URI outbreaks. The virus establishes a latent (dormant) infection in nerve tissue and reactivates during stress. Signs: watery to mucoid discharge, squinting, sometimes corneal ulcers.
- **Feline Calicivirus (FCV):** Less commonly causes eye signs than herpesvirus; more associated with oral ulcers and limping ("limping syndrome").
- **Chlamydophila felis:** A bacterial pathogen that causes conjunctivitis, often starting in one eye and spreading to the other. Responds well to tetracycline-class antibiotics (doxycycline).
- **Bacterial secondary infection:** Follows viral damage; discharge becomes purulent (yellow-green, thick).
- **Allergies or irritants:** Household chemicals, smoke, or dusty litter can cause mild bilateral conjunctivitis.

## Corneal Ulcers
section: causes

A corneal ulcer is a defect (scratch or erosion) in the surface of the cornea. It is painful, and affected cats will squint, tear excessively, and paw at the eye. The cornea may appear slightly cloudy or hazy in the area of the ulcer.

**Causes:** Trauma (cat scratch or fight), FHV-1 infection, foreign body, self-trauma (scratching the eye).
**Diagnosis:** A vet applies fluorescein dye — the dye stains ulcerated areas bright green under UV light.
**Treatment:** Topical antibiotic eye drops to prevent secondary infection, antiviral therapy (trifluridine or cidofovir drops) if herpesvirus-associated, pain management, and an Elizabethan collar to prevent self-trauma. **Topical steroids are absolutely contraindicated with corneal ulcers** — they inhibit healing and can lead to corneal perforation.

## Uveitis (Intraocular Inflammation)
section: causes

Uveitis is inflammation inside the eye. It is painful and can progress rapidly to glaucoma or permanent blindness.

**Signs:** The affected eye appears darker than the other (the iris changes colour/texture), the pupil may be constricted (miosis) and fail to respond to light normally, the eye may appear cloudy, and there is often pain (squinting, tearing).

**Causes in cats:** FeLV (feline leukaemia virus), FIV (feline immunodeficiency virus), FIP (feline infectious peritonitis), Toxoplasma gondii, lymphoma, high blood pressure (hypertension) — the latter is a very common cause of acute blindness in senior cats.

**Treatment:** This is a veterinary emergency — prompt treatment with topical and systemic anti-inflammatory medications and addressing the underlying cause can preserve vision.

## Hypertensive Retinopathy — Sudden Blindness in Senior Cats
section: emergency

This is an important and frequently missed emergency in older cats. Chronic high blood pressure (hypertension), usually secondary to CKD or hyperthyroidism, damages the tiny blood vessels supplying the retina. The retina can detach suddenly and completely, causing acute, complete blindness — often within hours.

**Signs:**
- Sudden onset of apparent blindness — the cat bumps into furniture, seems confused, cannot navigate familiar spaces
- Very large, dilated pupils that do not respond to a bright light (fixed, dilated pupils)
- Possible blood visible inside the eye (hyphaema)
- Often occurs in a cat already known to have CKD or hyperthyroidism

**This is an emergency.** Immediate blood pressure reduction with amlodipine can allow partial retinal reattachment and recovery of vision if treated within 12–24 hours. After 24–48 hours, the blindness is likely permanent.

## Home Eye Care
section: treatment

For mild discharge on otherwise normal-appearing eyes:
- Wipe gently with a soft, damp cloth (use a separate cloth for each eye to avoid cross-contamination).
- Use sterile saline (contact lens saline with no preservatives) on a cotton ball — do not use tap water directly.
- Never share eye drops between pets or use human eye drops without vet advice.

## When to See a Vet
section: emergency

This is an emergency (same-day care) if:
- The cat has suddenly gone blind or appears visually disoriented.
- One or both pupils are fixed and dilated and do not respond to bright light.
- There is blood visible inside the eye or a visible laceration of the eye itself.
- The eye appears to be bulging out (exophthalmos) or sunken in.
- Thick green/yellow discharge combined with a tightly closed eye and obvious pain.
- The eye is visibly perforated or a foreign body is embedded in it — do not attempt removal.

See a vet within 24–48 hours if:
- Discharge has continued for more than 2–3 days despite home cleaning.
- One eye is smaller or more sunken than the other (Horner's syndrome or ocular disease).
- The third eyelid has been prominently visible for more than 24 hours.
- Any cloudiness or opacity of the cornea.

## Prevention
section: prevention

- Keep the FVRCP vaccine (which covers feline herpesvirus and calicivirus) up to date.
- Control stress — herpesvirus reactivates during stressful events; L-Lysine supplementation may reduce severity of flares.
- Screen cats over 7 years of age for hypertension annually — a simple non-invasive blood pressure measurement that takes minutes.
- Treat CKD and hyperthyroidism promptly to prevent hypertensive retinal damage.
- Keep nails trimmed to reduce ocular trauma risk from accidental scratches during play.
