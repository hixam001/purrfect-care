# Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) in Cats

## Overview

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD), also called chronic renal failure, is one of the most common diseases in older cats. It is a progressive condition where the kidneys gradually lose their ability to filter waste products from the blood. CKD is not curable but is highly manageable, and cats can live comfortably for years with proper care. It is most common in cats over 7 years old, with prevalence increasing sharply in cats over 15.

---

## Symptoms
section: symptoms

CKD develops slowly. Early signs are often subtle:
- Increased thirst and urination (the kidneys can no longer concentrate urine)
- Weight loss and muscle wasting, especially along the spine
- Decreased appetite or complete refusal to eat
- Vomiting and nausea (from waste product buildup in the blood)
- Lethargy and weakness
- Dull, unkempt coat and poor grooming
- Bad breath with an ammonia or chemical smell (uraemic breath)
- Mouth ulcers or drooling
- Pale gums (from anaemia)
- High blood pressure (may cause sudden blindness)
- Constipation

## Home Management Steps
section: treatment

If your cat has been diagnosed with CKD:
1. Feed a kidney-specific prescription diet — these are low in phosphorus, which slows disease progression significantly.
2. Ensure maximum hydration: offer multiple water bowls around the house, use a cat water fountain, and feed predominantly wet food.
3. Administer any medications exactly as prescribed (e.g., phosphate binders, anti-nausea drugs, blood pressure medication).
4. Give subcutaneous (under-the-skin) fluids at home if your vet has shown you how — this is one of the most impactful supportive treatments.
5. Monitor your cat's weight weekly; sudden weight loss requires immediate vet contact.
6. Schedule regular vet check-ups (every 3-6 months) to monitor kidney values (creatinine, BUN, SDMA) and blood pressure.

## When to See a Vet Immediately
section: emergency

Seek emergency care if:
- The cat suddenly stops eating for more than 24-48 hours.
- There is sudden onset of blindness (a sign of hypertensive crisis from high blood pressure).
- The cat has repeated vomiting and cannot hold water down.
- The cat collapses, has seizures, or is completely unresponsive.
- You notice a dramatic increase in weakness or the cat cannot walk normally.

## Treatment Options (Vet-Prescribed)
section: treatment

- **Prescription renal diet**: Hill's k/d, Royal Canin Renal, or Purina NF — low phosphorus, controlled protein.
- **Phosphate binders**: Aluminum hydroxide or calcium carbonate mixed in food.
- **Anti-nausea medication**: Maropitant (Cerenia) or ondansetron.
- **Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents**: For anaemia related to CKD.
- **Amlodipine**: For hypertension (high blood pressure).
- **Subcutaneous fluids**: Given at home or at the clinic to maintain hydration.
- **Potassium supplementation**: If blood potassium levels are low.

---

## Prevention
section: prevention

- Schedule annual blood and urine tests for cats over 7 years old to catch CKD early (SDMA is the earliest marker).
- Feed high-quality, moisture-rich food throughout your cat's life.
- Ensure your cat is always well-hydrated.
- Avoid giving human NSAIDs (ibuprofen, aspirin) to cats — they are highly nephrotoxic and can cause acute kidney failure.
