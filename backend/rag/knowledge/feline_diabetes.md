# Feline Diabetes Mellitus

## Overview

Feline diabetes mellitus is one of the most common endocrine diseases in cats. It occurs when the pancreas does not produce enough insulin (Type 1), or the body's cells don't respond properly to insulin (Type 2 — most common in cats). Obese cats, middle-aged to older cats, and neutered male cats are at highest risk. With proper management, many diabetic cats can go into remission.

---

## Symptoms
section: symptoms

Classic signs of feline diabetes include:
- Excessive thirst (polydipsia) — constantly drinking from water bowls, taps, or unusual sources
- Frequent and excessive urination (polyuria) — may overflow the litter box
- Increased appetite (polyphagia) despite weight loss
- Unexplained weight loss even though the cat is eating well
- Weakness or difficulty walking, especially in the hind legs (diabetic neuropathy)
- Plantigrade stance — the cat walks on its hocks (heels) rather than its toes
- Lethargy and reduced grooming
- Dull, unkempt coat
- Vomiting (especially in diabetic ketoacidosis)
- Fruity or sweet smell on the breath (acetone smell — sign of ketoacidosis, an emergency)

## Home Monitoring Steps
section: treatment

If your cat has been diagnosed with diabetes:
1. Administer insulin injections exactly as prescribed by your vet — typically twice daily, after meals.
2. Feed a consistent, high-protein, low-carbohydrate diet. Avoid dry kibble; wet food is strongly preferred.
3. Monitor your cat's water intake and litter box usage daily — changes signal blood sugar fluctuations.
4. Keep a daily log of insulin doses, food intake, and any behavioral changes to share with your vet.
5. Learn to use a glucometer to test your cat's blood glucose at home (from the ear pinnae or paw pad).
6. Never skip or double an insulin dose without consulting your vet.

## When to See a Vet Immediately
section: emergency

Go to an emergency vet immediately if:
- Your cat shows signs of hypoglycemia (low blood sugar): sudden weakness, tremors, seizures, or collapse. Rub corn syrup or honey on their gums and go to the vet.
- You smell a sweet or fruity (acetone-like) odor on the cat's breath — this signals diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), a life-threatening emergency.
- The cat is vomiting repeatedly and refuses to eat.
- The cat cannot stand or walk properly.
- You accidentally gave a double dose of insulin.

## Treatment Options (Vet-Prescribed)
section: treatment

- **Insulin therapy**: Glargine (Lantus) or ProZinc insulin injected subcutaneously, typically twice daily.
- **Dietary management**: High-protein, low-carbohydrate prescription diets (e.g., Hill's m/d, Purina DM).
- **Regular glucose monitoring**: Fructosamine tests at the vet every 1-3 months.
- **Weight management**: Gradual weight loss through diet and light play to improve insulin sensitivity.

---

## Prevention
section: prevention

- Maintain a healthy weight — obesity is the single biggest risk factor.
- Feed a high-protein, low-carbohydrate diet (wet food over dry food).
- Provide regular play and exercise to improve insulin sensitivity.
- Schedule annual vet check-ups for cats over 7 years old.
