## Overview
section: overview

Ear problems are very common in cats and range from mild irritation and ear mites to serious deep infections involving the middle and inner ear. Cats have a long, narrow, L-shaped ear canal that is prone to trapping debris, wax, and moisture. The two most common presentations are otitis externa (infection or inflammation of the outer ear canal) and ear mite infestation (Otodectes cynotis). Head-shaking, pawing or scratching at the ear, tilting of the head, and odour from the ear are the classical signs.

---

## Ear Mites (Otodectes cynotis)
section: causes

Ear mites are the most common cause of ear disease in kittens and young cats, though they affect cats of any age. They are highly contagious between cats, dogs, and rabbits but do not infest humans.

**Signs of ear mites:**
- Intense scratching at both ears simultaneously
- Vigorous, frequent head-shaking
- Dark brown-to-black, dry, crumbly discharge in the ears that resembles coffee grounds
- Redness and inflammation at the ear opening
- Possible secondary skin lesions around the ear and on the neck from scratching

**Diagnosis and treatment:**
A vet will swab the ear and examine the discharge under a microscope — adult mites are visible as tiny white moving specks. Treatment: a single-dose topical antiparasitic (selamectin, moxidectin, or milbemycin oxime) applied to the skin. Prescription ear drops (ivermectin-based) are also effective. All pets in the household must be treated simultaneously.

## Bacterial and Yeast Ear Infections (Otitis Externa)
section: causes

Unlike dogs, primary bacterial ear infections are less common in cats. When they do occur, there is usually an underlying cause:
- Allergy (food allergy or environmental atopy) — the most common predisposing factor
- Foreign body (e.g., grass seed embedded in the canal)
- Polyp or mass in the ear canal
- Hypothyroidism or other hormonal disorder (rare in cats)
- Chronic moisture (rare in cats that don't swim, unlike dogs)

**Signs of otitis externa:**
- Discharge that is yellow, brown, green, or waxy (not the dry crumbly type of mites)
- Unpleasant odour from the ear
- Redness and swelling of the canal opening
- Pain on touching the ear — the cat may cry, flinch, or bite
- Usually affects one ear more than the other (mites tend to be bilateral/both ears)
- Head-shaking and scratching

## Inner Ear Disease (Otitis Media and Interna)
section: causes

Infection of the middle ear (otitis media) and inner ear (otitis interna) are more serious. They often result from:
- Extension of untreated outer ear infection
- Nasopharyngeal polyps — benign growths arising from the middle ear or Eustachian tube, common in young cats
- Upper respiratory tract infection spreading via the Eustachian tube

**Signs of inner ear disease:**
- Persistent head tilt (the cat holds its head at an angle, often to the affected side)
- Loss of balance, falling, rolling, or circling
- Nystagmus (rapid, involuntary back-and-forth movement of the eyes)
- Drooping of the face or eyelid on the affected side (Horner's syndrome)
- Deafness (partial or complete) in the affected ear

## Home Assessment
section: assessment

Gently examine the visible part of the ear (do not insert anything into the canal):
1. Look at the ear flap and opening: is there redness, swelling, discharge, or crusting?
2. Smell the ear: a yeasty, sweet, or foul odour indicates infection.
3. Touch the base of the ear gently: does the cat flinch or cry? Pain indicates infection.
4. Observe: Is the head tilted? Is the cat losing balance or showing abnormal eye movements?
5. Check both ears and compare: mite infestations are usually bilateral; infections often unilateral.

## When to See a Vet
section: emergency

See a vet urgently (same day) if:
- The cat has a pronounced head tilt, is losing balance, or has abnormal eye movements.
- There is significant swelling causing the ear flap to be puffed up (aural haematoma).
- The cat is in obvious pain and won't allow you to approach its head.
- Discharge is profuse, has blood, or has a very strong foul odour.
- A kitten is severely scratching both ears — untreated mite infestations can cause secondary infections and scarring.

See a vet within 2–3 days if:
- Mild scratching and dark debris in both ears (likely mites but needs confirmation).
- Mild discharge and odour without pain or balance issues.

## Home Ear Care
section: treatment

Ear cleaning at home (only for routine maintenance, not for active infections):
1. Use only a vet-approved ear cleaner — never use cotton buds/Q-tips inside the canal.
2. Apply a few drops of cleaner to the canal, gently massage the base of the ear for 20–30 seconds.
3. Allow the cat to shake its head, then wipe the outer canal and flap with a cotton ball.
4. Do not clean deeper than you can see.

**Never use:**
- Hydrogen peroxide or alcohol (cause irritation and damage)
- Olive oil or coconut oil (create an ideal environment for yeast)
- Cotton buds/Q-tips inside the canal (push debris deeper and can rupture the eardrum)

## Prevention
section: prevention

- Keep up with monthly broad-spectrum antiparasitic treatments (e.g., Revolution/Stronghold which covers ear mites).
- Check and gently clean ears monthly as part of your grooming routine.
- If your cat has recurrent ear infections, investigate for underlying allergies with your vet.
- Treat all pets in the household simultaneously to prevent re-infestation with mites.
