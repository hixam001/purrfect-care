## Overview
section: overview

Dental disease is the most prevalent health problem in cats — studies consistently show that over 70% of cats over three years of age have some degree of dental disease. Yet it is also one of the most frequently overlooked by owners, because cats rarely show obvious pain signals. The major dental conditions in cats are periodontal disease (gum disease), tooth resorption (formerly called feline odontoclastic resorptive lesions, FORL), stomatitis (severe oral inflammation), and fractured teeth. All cause significant pain and impact quality of life profoundly.

## Common Dental Conditions
section: causes

**1. Periodontal disease:**
Plaque (bacterial biofilm) builds up on the tooth surface within 24 hours of a dental clean. If not removed, it mineralises into tartar (calculus) within 3–5 days. Tartar causes gum inflammation (gingivitis), and over time the infection spreads below the gum line, destroying the periodontal ligament and jawbone (periodontitis). This is painful, causes tooth loss, and bacteria enter the bloodstream, potentially damaging the heart, kidneys, and liver.

**2. Tooth Resorption (TR/FORL):**
The second most common dental disease in cats after periodontal disease, affecting over 50% of cats at some point in their lives. The body's own cells (odontoclasts) begin to dissolve the tooth root from the inside. The cause is unknown. The lesion often starts at the gum line and is excruciatingly painful. Affected cats may chew on one side, drop food, chatter when touched near the face, or show no obvious signs at all. The only treatment is extraction of the affected tooth.

**3. Feline Chronic Gingivostomatitis (FCGS):**
A severe, painful immune-mediated condition where the entire oral mucosa becomes intensely inflamed and ulcerated. The gums bleed on touch, the breath is extremely foul, and the cat is in constant severe pain. Eating becomes difficult or impossible. The cause is not fully understood but involves a dysregulated immune response, often triggered by calicivirus infection. Treatment often requires full-mouth tooth extraction, which resolves the condition in up to 60% of affected cats.

**4. Fractured teeth:**
Most common in the upper canine teeth (fangs). Fractures may expose the pulp chamber (the nerve), causing intense, persistent pain and potential tooth root abscess. Outdoor cats that fight or chew hard objects are at highest risk.

## Signs of Dental Pain in Cats
section: assessment

Cats instinctively hide pain. Watch for subtle signs:
- **Eating changes:** Preferring soft food, eating only on one side of the mouth, dropping food, eating more slowly, or suddenly refusing hard kibble.
- **Drooling:** Especially if the saliva is blood-tinged.
- **Pawing at the mouth or face:** Particularly after eating.
- **Chattering:** A rapid jaw movement, often when touching the face near the affected tooth.
- **Bad breath (halitosis):** Noticeable even from a distance; often the first sign owners detect.
- **Reduced grooming:** Mouth pain causes cats to groom less, leading to a dull, unkempt coat.
- **Behavioural changes:** Increased irritability, hiding, reluctance to be touched around the head.
- **Weight loss:** Secondary to pain-induced reduced food intake.

## Home Oral Assessment
section: assessment

With the cat calm and cooperative:
1. Gently lift the lips and look at the gum line — are the gums pink and firm, or red and swollen?
2. Look at the tooth surfaces — is there yellow-brown calculus (tartar) present?
3. Smell the breath — mild fish odour is normal; a very strong foul or sweet smell is not.
4. Look at the gum-tooth junction closely — small pink fleshy growths at this junction may be tooth resorption lesions.
5. Do NOT force the mouth open if the cat resists — a struggling cat can injure itself and you.

## When to See a Vet
section: emergency

See a vet urgently if:
- The cat has stopped eating for more than 24 hours, especially alongside drooling or pawing at the mouth.
- There is bleeding from the mouth.
- A tooth is visibly fractured or hanging loose.
- The face is swollen (may indicate a tooth root abscess).
- The cat cannot close its mouth properly.

Routine vet dental check: any cat over 2 years should have an oral examination at every annual visit. Most cats need a professional dental cleaning (performed under general anaesthesia) every 1–3 years.

## Treatment
section: treatment

- **Professional dental cleaning (COHAT):** Performed under general anaesthesia, including supra- and subgingival scaling, polishing, full-mouth dental radiographs, and probing. This is the only way to properly assess and treat dental disease.
- **Extractions:** For teeth with severe disease, FORL, or fractures. Cats adapt extremely well to having teeth removed — they eat comfortably and are far more comfortable than before extraction.
- **Antibiotics:** Short course for dental abscesses before extraction; not a substitute for extraction.
- **Pain management:** Meloxicam (feline NSAID) and/or buprenorphine perioperatively.
- **FCGS treatment:** Immunosuppressive therapy (cyclosporine) as a temporary measure; full-mouth extraction as definitive treatment.

## Home Prevention
section: prevention

- **Tooth brushing:** The gold standard. Use a soft-bristle toothbrush or finger brush with veterinary enzymatic toothpaste (never human toothpaste — xylitol is toxic to pets). Start slowly, brush daily, ideally from kittenhood.
- **Dental chews and treats:** VOHC (Veterinary Oral Health Council) approved products have proven efficacy.
- **Water additives:** Chlorhexidine or xylitol-free dental additives reduce bacterial load.
- **Dental diets:** Hill's Prescription Diet t/d is VOHC approved and mechanically cleans teeth.
- **Regular professional cleanings:** The frequency depends on the individual cat's plaque accumulation rate, typically every 1–3 years.
