## Overview
section: overview

Preventive healthcare is the cornerstone of feline longevity. Vaccination, parasite control, regular wellness examinations, dental care, and appropriate nutrition collectively prevent the vast majority of serious cat illnesses. The AAFP vaccination guidelines divide vaccines into core vaccines (recommended for all cats regardless of lifestyle) and non-core vaccines (recommended based on individual risk factors and lifestyle). Understanding which vaccines your cat needs and when is essential for responsible cat ownership.

---

## Core Vaccines (All Cats)
section: prevention

Core vaccines protect against diseases that are ubiquitous, highly infectious, and/or cause severe illness regardless of whether the cat is indoor-only or outdoor.

**1. FVRCP — Feline Viral Rhinotracheitis, Calicivirus, and Panleukopenia (Cat Flu + Distemper):**
Often called the "3-in-1" or "cat flu" vaccine. Protects against:
- **Feline Herpesvirus-1 (FHV-1):** The major cause of feline upper respiratory infection; causes sneezing, nasal discharge, conjunctivitis, and potentially severe corneal ulcers. Establishes lifelong latent infection — stress causes reactivation.
- **Feline Calicivirus (FCV):** Upper respiratory signs, oral ulcers, and the severe "virulent systemic calicivirus" strain that causes fatal haemorrhagic disease.
- **Feline Panleukopenia (FPV):** Also called feline distemper or feline parvovirus. Destroys rapidly dividing cells — the intestinal lining, bone marrow, and developing foetus. Untreated, it has a mortality rate exceeding 90% in kittens. Resistant to most disinfectants and persists in the environment for years.

**Kitten schedule:**
- First dose at 6–8 weeks of age
- Booster every 3–4 weeks until 16 weeks of age (typically 3–4 total doses)
- Booster at 1 year (12 months after the last kitten vaccine)
- Then every 3 years for adult cats (core FVRCP titre can last 3 years or longer)

**2. Rabies vaccine:**
Core in most countries where rabies is endemic. Required by law in many regions.
- First dose at 12–16 weeks
- Booster 1 year later
- Then every 1–3 years depending on the product and local regulations
- In Pakistan and South Asia, rabies is endemic — vaccination is strongly recommended for cats with any outdoor access or contact with wildlife.

## Non-Core Vaccines (Based on Risk)
section: prevention

**FeLV (Feline Leukaemia Virus):**
Strongly recommended for cats with outdoor access or in multi-cat households where FeLV status is unknown. FeLV suppresses the immune system and predisposes cats to lymphoma, anaemia, and fatal secondary infections. It is transmitted via saliva (mutual grooming, shared food bowls, bite wounds) and from queen to kittens.
- Test all cats for FeLV before vaccination (vaccinating an FeLV-positive cat provides no benefit)
- 2 doses 3–4 weeks apart, then annually for at-risk cats
- Once fully indoor with no new cat introductions, annual booster can be reconsidered

**FIV (Feline Immunodeficiency Virus):**
Vaccine available in some countries. FIV is transmitted primarily through bite wounds — outdoor cats that fight are highest risk. The licensed FIV vaccine has efficacy limitations and may interfere with FIV antibody test results, so its use is controversial. Focus on preventing exposure (indoor lifestyle, neutering).

**Bordetella bronchiseptica:**
Recommended for cats entering boarding facilities, shelters, or multi-cat households with recent respiratory outbreaks.

**Feline Infectious Peritonitis (FIP):**
No commercially available vaccine with proven efficacy in most markets. Focus on biosecurity.

## Preventive Healthcare by Life Stage
section: prevention

**Kittens (0–6 months):**
- Deworming: every 2 weeks from 2–8 weeks, then monthly until 6 months
- Flea prevention: monthly topical/oral treatment from 8 weeks
- FVRCP series (see above)
- FeLV vaccination if at risk
- Rabies vaccination from 12–16 weeks
- Spay/neuter: from 4–6 months (earlier is better — before first heat eliminates mammary cancer risk almost entirely)
- Microchipping: at time of first vet visit or spay/neuter

**Adult cats (1–7 years):**
- Annual wellness examination: weight, dental scoring, BCS assessment, blood pressure (from age 5+)
- FVRCP booster every 3 years (unless high-risk environment)
- FeLV booster annually if at risk
- Rabies: per local regulations
- Annual or biannual faecal parasite testing
- Monthly flea/parasite prevention

**Senior cats (7–10 years) and geriatric cats (over 10 years):**
- Biannual wellness examinations — disease progresses much faster in seniors
- Annual blood panel: full biochemistry, CBC, T4 (thyroid), and urinalysis — this single step catches most major senior diseases (CKD, hyperthyroidism, diabetes, anaemia) while they are still manageable
- Blood pressure measurement: annual from age 7; biannual if on treatment or hypertensive
- Dental cleanings: likely more frequently; annual X-rays during cleaning
- Nutritional assessment: senior cats lose muscle mass (sarcopaenia) — high-protein diets are important

## Parasite Control Schedule
section: prevention

A practical monthly prevention protocol for most cats:

**Fleas and ticks:** Monthly topical isoxazoline (Bravecto — also covers ear mites and some ticks, lasts 3 months), selamectin (Revolution — also covers heartworm, ear mites), or fipronil/metaflumizone (e.g., Frontline, Broadline).

**Intestinal worms:** Quarterly deworming with praziquantel + pyrantel or fenbendazole for outdoor and hunting cats. Annual faecal test and deworm as needed for indoor-only cats.

**Heartworm:** In heartworm-endemic regions (Asia, tropics), monthly prevention with selamectin or moxidectin is recommended. Cats are aberrant heartworm hosts but can develop serious disease with even a single worm.

## Microchipping
section: prevention

A microchip (ISO 11784/11785 standard, 15-digit, 134.2 kHz frequency) is a permanent form of identification injected under the skin between the shoulder blades. It does not act as a GPS tracker — it requires a scanner to read. Benefits:
- Permanent link between you and your cat that cannot be lost like a collar
- Required for international travel (PETS passport system)
- Dramatically increases the chance of being reunited with a lost cat
- Procedure takes seconds, is minimally painful, and lasts the cat's lifetime
- Register and keep your contact details up to date with the microchip registry

## Spaying and Neutering
section: prevention

**Female cats (spaying / ovariohysterectomy or ovariectomy):**
- Eliminates the risk of pyometra (infected uterus — a common emergency in intact females)
- Eliminates the risk of ovarian cancer
- Reduces mammary (breast) cancer risk by over 90% if done before the first heat; by 86% if done before the second heat
- Prevents unwanted pregnancies
- Eliminates calling and associated behavioural stress during heat cycles

**Male cats (neutering / castration):**
- Eliminates testicular cancer risk
- Significantly reduces roaming, fighting, and associated injury and FIV transmission risk
- Virtually eliminates spraying (urine marking) if done before the behaviour is established
- Reduces aggression toward other cats

**Metabolic effect of neutering:** Calorie requirements drop by approximately 25–30% after neutering. Adjust food portions immediately to prevent post-neutering obesity.
